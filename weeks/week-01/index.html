SOC Operations and Analyst Thinking

Before you touch a single tool, you need to think like the person whose job depends on getting this right at 3 a.m., with an incomplete picture, and a business that is losing money for every minute the decision takes.

🎯 Focus: Analyst reasoning, not platforms
🧩 Deliverable: Alert-triage sheet
🖥️ Infra: None, pen, paper, and your head
Orientation

Why we start here, and not with a tool

Every mentee wants to open Wazuh in Week 1. We are deliberately not doing that, and by the end of this section you'll understand why that's not a delay, it's the actual foundation.

Picture two analysts. Both are looking at the exact same alert on the exact same screen. Analyst A has been taught tools, she knows every button in the SIEM, can write a query fast, and can navigate five different dashboards without thinking. Analyst B has been taught to think, she's slower on the keyboard, but every time she looks at something, she is silently asking herself: is this real? What would it mean if it were? What don't I know yet? What's the cost of being wrong in either direction?

Six months into a real job, Analyst B outperforms Analyst A by a wide margin, not because she knows more shortcuts, but because tools only ever answer the questions you know to ask. A SOC analyst's real product isn't "alerts closed per shift." It's defensible decisions under uncertainty. That is a thinking skill, and like any thinking skill, it's trainable, which is exactly what this week does.

A mentor's honest take

If you ever interview a junior analyst and ask "what would you do with this alert?" and they immediately reach for a tool name as the answer, that's a signal they were trained on software, not on judgement. The best answer to "what would you do?" is always a question back: what do we actually know so far, and what would tell us more? That instinct is what Week 1 is trying to build in you before any tool exists to hide behind.

Here's the uncomfortable truth about most junior SOC hires globally, not just in Nigeria or Africa: they can recite the definition of a "false positive" in an interview, but the moment they're handed a real, messy, ambiguous alert queue, they freeze, or worse, they rubber-stamp everything as "benign" just to clear the queue. This week exists to break that pattern before it ever forms.

Objectives

By the end of this week, you will be able to

  • Explain, in your own words, the practical difference between an event, an alert, and an incident, and correctly classify a real example into one of the three.
  • Distinguish a true positive from a false positive from a benign true positive, a category most junior analysts have never heard of and desperately need.
  • Assign severity, priority, and confidence to an alert independently of each other, and explain why conflating them is a common and costly mistake.
  • Walk through a structured triage decision process on an unfamiliar alert without being told the answer in advance.
  • Write an analyst note that another analyst, who knows nothing about the case, could pick up and continue from, cold.
  • Explain what SLA, MTTA, and MTTR actually measure, why a business cares about them, and how they can be gamed dishonestly (and why that's dangerous).
  • Produce a complete, professional alert-triage sheet for a queue of mixed alerts, this week's graded deliverable.
The Big Picture

What a SOC actually is, underneath the acronym

Strip away the marketing language and a Security Operations Centre is a fairly simple machine with one job: turn an overwhelming amount of noise into a small number of decisions worth a human's attention.

A modern mid-sized organisation can generate tens of millions of log events per day, every login, every file open, every DNS lookup, every firewall decision. A SOC's entire reason for existing is to compress that firehose down into something a team of humans can actually act on. The pipeline looks like this:

A representative daily funnel for a mid-sized organisation. The exact numbers vary wildly by industry and maturity, the shape never does.

Notice what this diagram is really telling you: the SOC's value is created almost entirely in the narrowing, not the collecting. Any company can buy a tool that ingests 20 million events a day. What separates a functioning SOC from an expensive noise machine is the human judgement that turns 300 alerts into the 1 or 2 that actually deserve an incident response. That judgement is what this whole 20-week programme is building in you, and it starts with the raw thinking skill this week teaches, before any tool automates part of the funnel for you.

The three functions every SOC performs, no matter its size or budget

FunctionWhat it actually meansWho typically does it
DetectNotice that something worth looking at has happened, from the sea of everything that happenedTier 1 / Level 1 analysts, automated rules
InvestigateEstablish what actually occurred, its scope, and whether it's realTier 2 analysts, threat hunters
RespondContain, remediate, and communicate the outcome to the businessIncident responders, sometimes Tier 2/3, management

This programme deliberately trains you across all three, because in the real job market, especially in smaller or growing SOCs, which is where most of you will start, the line between these tiers is blurry. You will frequently be asked to do all three in a single shift. A narrow specialist who can only "detect" is far less employable than someone who can carry a case from first alert to closed incident.

Core Concept 1

Event, Alert, Incident: the three words you must never confuse again

This sounds basic. It is not. Confusing these three words is one of the most common reasons junior analysts either panic unnecessarily or, worse, fail to escalate something serious.

Each layer is a subset of the one above it. Every incident was once an alert; almost no event ever becomes one.

Event

An event is simply a recorded occurrence. A user logging in. A file being opened. A DNS query being made. A firewall allowing a connection. Your Windows machine alone generates thousands of these per hour just from you existing on it. An event, by itself, carries almost no meaning, it's a fact, not a judgement.

example, raw Windows Security event
EventID: 4624 (An account was successfully logged on) Time: 2026-08-14 09:14:02 Account: CHIDI.OKAFOR Logon Type: 3 (Network) Source IP: 10.20.4.15 Workstation: FIN-WK-018

By itself, this event tells you almost nothing actionable. Chidi logs in from that workstation every single working day at roughly that time. This is an event, not the event that matters.

Alert

An alert is what you get when a detection rule, written by a human, running automatically, decides that a specific event, or a pattern of events, crosses a threshold worth flagging. An alert is a claim, not a fact. It says "this might matter," not "this does matter." That distinction is the entire reason triage exists as a discipline.

example, SIEM-generated alert
[ALERT] Rule: Impossible Travel: Same Account, Two Countries Account: CHIDI.OKAFOR First logon: 09:14:02, Lagos, NG (10.20.4.15, internal) Second logon: 09:26:41, Amsterdam, NL (185.220.101.7, external VPN exit node) Time delta: 12 minutes 39 seconds Distance: ~5,100 km, physically impossible in that window Severity: HIGH (auto-assigned) · Status: NEW

Now the same underlying account activity means something, because a correlation rule connected two events that, alone, meant nothing. This alert deserves an analyst's attention. It does not yet deserve to be called an incident, you don't know yet if it's a compromised account, a misconfigured VPN client, a travelling employee whose corporate laptop cached credentials, or a false geolocation from a shared NAT exit node.

Incident

An incident is what an alert becomes after a human has investigated it and confirmed it represents real, adverse impact or a genuine, unmitigated risk that requires a coordinated response. Not every alert becomes an incident, in fact, in a healthy, well-tuned SOC, the overwhelming majority never do. An incident carries formal weight: it gets a case number, a timeline, an owner, and usually visibility above the SOC itself (management, sometimes legal, sometimes customers).

The mistake that gets people fired (or worse, gets a company breached)

Calling something an "incident" too early wastes the whole organisation's time and burns your credibility. Leadership stops trusting your escalations. Calling something "just an event" when it was actually the opening move of a real breach is far worse: it's how ransomware operators sit undetected in a network for weeks. The entire discipline of triage exists to make that judgement call correctly, consistently, under time pressure, with incomplete information. That is genuinely a hard skill, it is not supposed to feel obvious yet.

Quick self-test Click an answer

Classify each of the following as an Event, an Alert, or an Incident. Pick an answer before you scroll past it.

1. A firewall log line showing an outbound connection to port 443 was permitted.

Event. Outbound HTTPS is normal, high-volume traffic. On its own, a single permitted connection is just a recorded fact. No rule has judged it. No human has reviewed it.

2. Your EDR flags a PowerShell process spawning from Microsoft Word, and a rule fires because that parent-child relationship is unusual.

Alert. A detection rule made a judgement call that this pattern is suspicious enough to deserve human attention. It has not yet been confirmed as malicious. It could be a legitimate macro-based business process, though that would itself be worth flagging as risky practice.

3. After investigating alert #2, the analyst confirms the PowerShell process downloaded and executed a second-stage payload that established a connection to a known malicious IP, and multiple hosts show the same pattern.

Incident. A human has confirmed real, adverse activity with confirmed scope beyond a single host. This now needs a case number, a coordinated response, and almost certainly management visibility.

Core Concept 2

True positives, false positives, and the category nobody teaches you

Most training stops at two categories. Real SOC work needs four, and the missing two are exactly where junior analysts get it wrong.

CategoryMeaningReal example
TRUE POSITIVEThe alert fired correctly, AND the activity is genuinely malicious or unauthorised.An alert for a known malware hash fires, and the file is confirmed malicious on the host.
FALSE POSITIVEThe alert fired, but the activity is not what the rule assumed, the detection logic itself was wrong or too broad.A rule designed to catch "PowerShell downloading a file" fires on your organisation's legitimate Windows Update maintenance script.
BENIGN TRUE POSITIVEThe alert fired correctly, and the described activity genuinely happened exactly as detected, but it turns out to be authorised, expected, or otherwise harmless in this specific context.The "impossible travel" rule correctly detected two geographically distant logins for one account, because the second one was the employee connecting through a corporate VPN concentrator based in another country.
FALSE NEGATIVENo alert fired at all, even though malicious activity occurred, the most dangerous category, because nobody was even asked to look.An attacker uses a "living off the land" technique (a legitimate admin tool used maliciously) that no rule was written to catch.
Why "benign true positive" is the category that separates juniors from seasoned analysts

A junior analyst sees the VPN-travel alert, notices it's "not malicious," and writes "false positive" in the ticket. This is factually wrong, and it matters: if you mark it a false positive, someone eventually "fixes" the detection rule by weakening it, usually by excluding that VPN's IP range entirely. Six months later, an actual attacker routes through a compromised device that happens to sit on that same excluded IP range, and the rule that would have caught them was quietly disabled by a well-meaning but imprecise ticket months earlier. Calling it a benign true positive instead keeps the detection logic intact, and instead prompts a conversation about tuning context (e.g., "always require MFA-confirmed VPN sessions to suppress this alert" rather than "ignore this IP range forever").

Real-world grounding: this distinction has caused real breaches

In several well-documented breach post-mortems over the years, Target's 2013 breach among the most widely cited, investigators later confirmed that security tools did generate alerts related to the intrusion. The alerts were not acted on with appropriate urgency, partly because the team was buried under alert volume and partly because prior imprecise tuning had eroded trust in the alerting pipeline. The lesson for you, at Week 1, before you've even touched a SIEM: how you classify an alert has downstream consequences on the entire detection programme, not just on today's ticket. This is not an abstract concern, it is one of the most repeated root causes in breach retrospectives across the industry.

Core Concept 3

Severity, Priority, and Confidence: three different questions, not one

Junior analysts routinely treat these as synonyms. They are three completely independent axes, and conflating them leads to either dangerous complacency or paralysing overreaction.

  • Severity answers: "If this is real, how bad is the potential impact?", asked independent of whether you currently believe it's real.
  • Priority answers: "Given everything else on my plate right now, how soon does this need attention?", a scheduling decision, not a technical one.
  • Confidence answers: "How sure am I, right now, with the evidence I have, that this is genuinely malicious?"

Here is why keeping these separate matters so much: a ransomware precursor alert (a suspicious scheduled task that matches known pre-ransomware tradecraft) can have high severity (if real, this is catastrophic) with low confidence (you've only seen one weak indicator so far) and yet still deserve high priority, because the cost of being slow, if you're wrong about the low confidence, is unacceptable. A junior analyst who conflates these three often reasons "I'm not sure this is real (low confidence), so it's probably not that bad (wrongly inferred low severity), so I'll get to it later (wrongly low priority)", and that chain of reasoning is exactly how early ransomware indicators get missed.

Severity and confidence are independent axes. Priority is a decision you make using this map, not a third axis you guess directly.

Worked example: applying all three

alert queue entry
Rule: Suspicious LSASS Memory Access Host: HR-WK-004 (Human Resources department, domain-joined) Process: taskmgr.exe accessing lsass.exe memory Time: 2026-08-14 14:02:11 One prior similar alert on this host, 11 months ago, closed as benign (user opened Task Manager, right-clicked a process, chose "Create Dump File")

Severity: HIGH. LSASS memory access is a textbook credential-dumping technique (this maps to MITRE ATT&CK technique T1003.001, which you'll learn formally in Week 5); if this is real, an attacker could extract every cached credential on this machine.

Confidence (initial): MEDIUM-LOW, task manager legitimately touches LSASS memory in specific, known circumstances, and there's a documented benign precedent on this exact host.

Priority: HIGH, despite the moderate-low confidence, because the potential blast radius (severity) is too large to let sit in the queue while you investigate something with a "safer" profile. You escalate the investigation urgently precisely because you're not yet sure, not despite it.

Core Concept 4

Triage and escalation: a repeatable process, not a gut feeling

"Trust your gut" is bad advice for a Week 1 analyst, you don't have a trained gut yet. What you need instead is a process reliable enough to run even when you're tired, it's 3 a.m., and the queue has 40 alerts in it.

1

Read the whole alert before touching anything

Note the rule name, the host, the account, the timestamp, and any fields the SIEM surfaced automatically. Resist the urge to immediately search for the IP address, orient yourself in the alert itself first.

2

Establish context, what is "normal" for this asset and account?

Is this a server or a workstation? A finance department machine or a developer's laptop? Does this account normally log in at this hour, from this location, doing this kind of activity? Context is what turns a bare fact into a judgement.

3

Look for corroborating or contradicting evidence

Check related logs, did anything else unusual happen around the same time on this host or account? A single suspicious indicator is a hypothesis. Multiple aligned indicators are evidence.

4

Classify: true positive, false positive, or benign true positive

Make the call explicitly, and write down why, the reasoning is more valuable than the label itself, because the reasoning is what a reviewer or a future you will actually check.

5

Assign severity, confidence, and priority independently

Resist collapsing these into one number. Write all three down, even when they clearly agree with each other.

6

Decide: close, monitor, or escalate

If you escalate, say to whom and why, and hand over enough context that they don't have to redo your work. If you close, your closure note should let a future auditor understand your reasoning without needing to ask you.

Common pitfall: "alert fatigue" triage

When a queue has 80 alerts and 20 minutes left in a shift, the temptation is to skim and pattern-match to whatever was closed as benign yesterday. This is exactly how real incidents get missed. Attackers who study a target's environment sometimes deliberately generate noisy, obviously-benign-looking alerts first, hoping to desensitise the analyst before the real move. Speed is a skill you build over months of genuine practice, not a shortcut you take in Week 1 by skipping steps.

Core Concept 5

Evidence and analyst notes, writing for a stranger, not for yourself

The single habit that most separates a professional SOC from an amateur one is documentation discipline. This is not bureaucracy, it's the mechanism that lets a 24/7 operation function across shift changes, sick days, and legal proceedings.

Write every analyst note as though the next person to read it has zero memory of this conversation, might be reading it under legal scrutiny six months from now, and needs to be able to continue your work without calling you. That single mental model, "write for a stranger", fixes almost every bad documentation habit before it forms.

analyst_note.txt, do not write like this
checked it, looks fine, user was probably just on vpn. closing.

What's wrong: no evidence cited, no reasoning shown, no indication of what "checked" even means, no timestamp of when the check happened, and "probably" with nothing backing it up. If this account is compromised next week, nobody, including you, can reconstruct what was actually verified here.

analyst_note.txt, the standard to aim for
2026-08-14 09:41 WAT, C.ADEYEMI, Tier 1 Alert: Impossible Travel, account CHIDI.OKAFOR (ref #SOC-11042) Checked VPN concentrator logs (Fortigate, VPN-01) for the 09:26:41 Amsterdam-sourced session: confirmed source IP 185.220.101.7 matches the corporate NL VPN exit node pool (range 185.220.101.0/24, documented in NetOps asset register, link: wiki/network/vpn-pools). Confirmed with C.OKAFOR directly via Teams at 09:38 that he connected via corporate VPN before his 09:14 badge-in; he was testing VPN failover per his manager's request (ref: change ticket CHG-2291). Classification: Benign True Positive. Detection logic worked correctly; underlying cause is authorised VPN testing. Recommendation: do NOT suppress this rule for this IP range, suggest adding an MFA-session correlation exception instead (see Week 7, detection tuning). Escalating that suggestion to Detection Engineering, not closing it silently. Status: CLOSED, Benign True Positive.

What's right: every claim is backed by a checked source, the human verification step is documented with a timestamp and a reference, the classification is explicit and correctly uses "benign true positive" rather than "false positive," and, critically, the analyst didn't quietly let the detection rule get weakened; they routed the tuning suggestion to the right place instead.

Core Concept 6

SLA, MTTA, MTTR: the numbers your manager actually gets measured on

You will hear these three acronyms constantly in a real SOC job. Understanding what they measure, and how they can be gamed, makes you noticeably more employable than someone who's only memorised the definitions.

TermFull nameWhat it measures
SLAService Level AgreementA committed target, often contractual, for how quickly certain severities must be acknowledged or resolved. E.g., "Critical alerts acknowledged within 15 minutes, 24/7."
MTTAMean Time To AcknowledgeThe average time between an alert firing and a human actually looking at it. Measures queue responsiveness.
MTTRMean Time To Respond / ResolveThe average time between an alert firing and the case being fully closed (contained, remediated, or dismissed with confidence). Measures end-to-end effectiveness.

Why does a business obsess over these numbers? Because time is the one resource an attacker needs and a defender is trying to deny them. The concept of "dwell time", how long an attacker sits undetected in a network, is one of the most quoted statistics in the entire industry precisely because it's the clearest proxy for "how good is this SOC, really." Industry breach reports over the years (IBM's annual Cost of a Data Breach report is a commonly cited source) have repeatedly shown that breaches identified and contained faster cost organisations dramatically less, in direct financial terms, than ones that take months to detect.

How these metrics get gamed, and why that's dangerous

A team under pressure to hit an aggressive MTTA target can technically "acknowledge" every alert within seconds by clicking it open and immediately clicking it closed, the metric improves while the actual investigative work never happened. This is a real, documented failure mode in under-resourced or badly-incentivised SOCs. As an analyst, understand that these numbers exist to serve good investigation, not to be optimised directly at the expense of it. If you ever find yourself rushing a real judgement call purely to protect a personal MTTA statistic, that's a sign the incentive structure around you needs to be raised with your lead, not a sign you should cut the corner quietly.

Core Concept 7

Case ownership and handover

Most SOCs run 24/7, which means no single analyst ever sees a case from start to finish. The quality of your handovers is, in a very real sense, the quality of your team's collective memory.

A case has a clear owner at every point in time, never "everyone," never "whoever picks it up next." When a shift ends with an open case, the outgoing analyst is responsible for making sure the incoming analyst can continue without a debrief call at 6 a.m. This is exactly the discipline your Week 3 case-management module will build on, and the discipline you'll practice again during the incident response and capstone weeks later in this programme, it starts as a habit here.

A realistic scenario

It's 23:50. Amara is closing out her shift and has one open, unresolved alert: a suspicious outbound connection from a finance server, moderate confidence, that she hasn't finished investigating. She could write "still checking this, seems weird" and hand off verbally to the night analyst who hasn't arrived yet. Instead, she writes a structured note: what she's checked, what she hasn't, her working hypothesis, and the exact next step she'd take if she had ten more minutes. At 00:15, Tunde starts his shift, reads the note, and picks up exactly where she left off, no phone call needed, no repeated work, no lost thirty minutes at the start of the night shift while a potentially live incident sits idle.

Applied Practice

Hands-on: a full triage walkthrough, worked start to finish

No lab is needed this week, but seeing exactly how a seasoned analyst reasons through a real queue entry, end to end, is what turns the theory above into something you can actually do.

Below is a realistic mixed queue of five alerts, exactly the kind you'll receive as this week's deliverable exercise (with a different, unseen set). Watch how each one gets worked, not just what the final answer is.

Alert Queue: Shift 09:00–17:00, 14 August 2026

SOC-11201
Rule: Repeated Failed Logon Followed by Success Account: SVC-BACKUP (service account) Failures: 47 over 6 minutes, then 1 success Source: 10.20.4.201 (internal, backup server subnet) Target: DC-02 (Domain Controller)
Make the call before you read on
Classification
Priority

Reasoning: Service accounts should never show 47 failed logons. They don't "mistype" passwords like humans do. That pattern is far more consistent with an automated password-guessing attempt, or a misconfigured scheduled job retrying with a stale credential.

Classification: Likely True Positive (pending confirmation)   Severity: HIGH (domain controller, service account, successful logon after brute force)   Confidence: MEDIUM   Priority: CRITICAL: escalate now

Next step written in the note: check what the service account did immediately after the successful logon; check whether a scheduled backup job's stored credential was recently rotated (common cause of exactly this pattern) before assuming malicious intent, though escalate to Tier 2 immediately regardless, given the target is a domain controller.

SOC-11202
Rule: New Member Added to Domain Admins Group Actor: IT.ADMIN.BISI New member: J.NWACHUKWU Time: 11:14:02 Change ticket referenced in event: CHG-2287
Make the call before you read on
Classification
Priority

Reasoning: Domain Admins membership changes are always high severity by nature of the privilege involved. This event carries a change ticket reference, which is exactly the kind of corroborating context that should be checked, not assumed.

Classification (after checking CHG-2287 in the change management system and confirming it's an approved, scheduled promotion): Benign True Positive   Severity: HIGH (by nature, Domain Admin is always high severity as a category)   Confidence: HIGH (confirmed against source-of-truth records)   Priority: LOW: document and close

Note the discipline: severity stays HIGH even though this closes quickly, because the category of action is inherently high-impact if it were ever unauthorised. A future reviewer scanning only by severity should still be able to find this event if needed.

SOC-11203
Rule: Periodic Beaconing Pattern: DNS Host: MKT-WK-032 Destination: analytics-cdn-edge7[.]net Interval: every 58–62 seconds, ±2s jitter Duration observed: 6 hours 40 minutes Volume: ~48 bytes per query (unusually small and consistent)
Make the call before you read on
Classification
Priority

Reasoning: Regular, tightly-timed beaconing with jittered intervals and small, consistent payload sizes is a classic command-and-control communication pattern. This is one of the signatures you'll learn to hunt for explicitly in Week 10 and Week 9 (Zeek/Suricata). The domain name is designed to look like a legitimate analytics/CDN service, which is itself a red flag: attackers deliberately choose plausible-sounding domains.

Classification: Suspected True Positive   Severity: HIGH   Confidence: MEDIUM (pattern is highly suggestive but domain hasn't yet been confirmed malicious against threat intel)   Priority: HIGH: escalate for hunting/enrichment

Correctly, this analyst does not wait for a threat intelligence lookup before escalating. The behavioural pattern alone is strong enough evidence to act on now, with enrichment (Week 13 onward) running in parallel, not as a gate before action.

SOC-11204
Rule: Large File Write to Removable Media Host: DESIGN-WK-011 User: T.BALOGUN (Graphic Designer) Volume: 2.3 GB written to USB device "SanDisk Ultra" Files: *.psd, *.ai, *.png (design assets) Time: 15:42 (within normal working hours)
Make the call before you read on
Classification
Priority

Reasoning: This user's role plausibly explains large design file transfers to removable media. Designers routinely move large asset files to USB drives for client delivery or print vendors. Context (role, file types, time of day) all align with expected behaviour.

Classification: Benign True Positive   Severity: MEDIUM (data exfiltration category is never "low" by default; it is contextually downgraded here, not dismissed by category)   Confidence: HIGH   Priority: LOW: document and close

A junior mistake here would be to close this as "false positive." The rule fired correctly and the described activity genuinely happened. It's benign, not false. Data-loss-prevention policy questions (should designers be allowed unencrypted USB transfer at all?) get raised separately with the policy team. That's not this analyst's call to make unilaterally, but it is worth noting in the ticket as an observation.

SOC-11205
Rule: Database Admin Console Access Outside Business Hours Account: DBA_KEMI Time: 02:14 WAT Action: Full export of CUSTOMERS table (412,000 rows) Source: Kemi's usual workstation, VPN not used, on-site badge log shows NO entry that night
Make the call before you read on
Classification
Priority

Reasoning: This is the alert designed to test whether you actually read every field, not just the headline rule name. The badge log discrepancy (no physical entry recorded, yet activity from her usual workstation) is the single most important detail in the whole alert, and it directly contradicts the simplest explanation ("she came in early to work").

Classification: True Positive (likely compromised credentials or workstation)   Severity: CRITICAL (412,000 customer records, a textbook data breach if confirmed)   Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH   Priority: CRITICAL: immediate escalation, do not wait for shift handover

This is the alert every analyst must get right. It's also a realistic illustration of why "context" (Core Concept 4, step 2) is not optional. The badge log is a data source most junior analysts wouldn't think to check on a database alert at all.

What this walkthrough was really testing

Notice that severity of the underlying category (Domain Admin changes, data exfiltration, database exports) stayed consistently high across alerts #2, #4 and #5, even though two of them closed as benign. Only confidence and priority moved based on context. If you found yourself wanting to mark #2 and #4 as "low severity" just because they turned out fine, go back and re-read Core Concept 3. That's the single most common reasoning error this week is designed to correct.

This Week's Deliverable

Professional alert-triage sheet

You will receive a mixed queue of benign, suspicious, and malicious alerts, without being told in advance which is which, deliberately mirroring the walkthrough above but with a fresh set of scenarios. For every alert in the queue, your triage sheet must contain:

  • Your classification: True Positive, False Positive, or Benign True Positive, with reasoning, not just the label.
  • Severity, Priority, and Confidence, assigned independently, each with a one-line justification.
  • Evidence referenced, what specifically in the alert (or context you'd check) supports your conclusion.
  • Your disposition: close, monitor, or escalate, and if escalating, to whom and with what specific next step.
  • An analyst note for at least two of the alerts, written to the "stranger" standard from Core Concept 5.

This sheet is graded on the quality and defensibility of your reasoning, not on whether you happened to land on the label an instructor had in mind. Two analysts can reasonably disagree on a borderline classification and both score well, provided their reasoning is sound and evidence-based.

Competency Checkpoint

What "passing" Week 1 actually looks like

You can make defensible first-line decisions on an unfamiliar alert without an instructor labelling it for you first. That means: given a genuinely new scenario you've never seen, you correctly identify what category of question you're answering (is it real? how bad? how sure? how urgent?), you know which evidence would change your answer, and you can write your reasoning down clearly enough that another analyst, or an auditor six months later, can follow exactly how you got there. Speed comes later. Correctness of reasoning is what Week 1 is building.

Reference

Glossary

Alert fatigue▾

The degraded judgement that sets in when an analyst is exposed to a high volume of low-value alerts over time, leading to faster but less careful triage, and a higher risk of missing genuinely important alerts buried in the noise.

Dwell time▾

The length of time an attacker remains present and undetected inside a compromised environment, from initial access to detection. One of the most closely watched industry metrics for SOC effectiveness.

Escalation▾

The act of formally handing an alert or case to a more senior analyst, a different team, or management, because it exceeds the current handler's authority, expertise, or the situation's urgency.

Detection rule / correlation rule▾

Logic, written by a human, that examines incoming events (or patterns across multiple events) and decides whether to generate an alert. You'll write these yourself starting Week 5.

Tier 1 / Tier 2 / Tier 3▾

A common (though not universal) SOC staffing structure. Tier 1 performs initial triage; Tier 2 conducts deeper investigation; Tier 3 handles the most complex cases, threat hunting, and detection engineering. Many smaller SOCs blend these tiers, this programme trains you across all of them.

Disposition▾

The final decision recorded against an alert or case: typically closed (with a reason), monitored, or escalated.

  • IBM's annual "Cost of a Data Breach" report, look at how detection/containment speed correlates with financial impact; a genuinely eye-opening read for understanding why MTTA/MTTR matter to a business, not just a SOC.
  • The Target Corporation 2013 breach retrospective, widely written about; a sobering, concrete case study in what happens when correct alerts don't translate into correct action.
  • MITRE ATT&CK framework (attack.mitre.org), you don't need to study it in depth yet (Week 5 covers it formally), but browsing it now will make the vocabulary in later weeks feel familiar rather than new.
  • Ask a working SOC analyst (if you know one) to describe their worst "we should have caught that sooner" story. Real anecdotes stick far better than any textbook example, including the ones in this document.
